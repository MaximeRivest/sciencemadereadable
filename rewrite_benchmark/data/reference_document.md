# Conversation rewrite — verbatim excerpts

## title

**How growers could get more edible pine nuts from pine cones**

## abstract

Stone pine trees produce pine nuts that people eat. In many places, the amount of edible nuts harvested from a given weight of cones has been falling. This worries growers because pine nuts are in high demand.

We studied 560 cones from seven tree plantations in Chile over ten years. We counted, measured, and weighed the cones, their seeds, and the edible nuts inside the seed shells. We also checked for damage. We compared heavy cones with light cones, using calculations that accounted for differences between places and years.

The Chilean cones weighed about 474 grams on average, compared with about 300 grams reported in places where these trees grow naturally. Heavy cones contained more seeds—about 125 each, compared with 89 in light cones. They also provided more edible nuts for each gram of cone: edible nuts made up 4.05% of the weight of heavy cones, compared with 3.62% for light cones. Heavy cones had fewer damaged seeds: 9.0%, compared with 15.9%.

The amount of food obtained from a cone was therefore linked to the size, weight, and health of its seeds and nuts. Watering trees and adding nutrients to the soil might help them produce heavier cones and more edible nuts, although this study did not directly test whether those treatments would cause that improvement.

## introduction_first

The stone pine, whose scientific name is *Pinus pinea*, is valued for its edible pine nuts. These nuts contain useful fats, protein, vitamins, minerals, and other substances that can affect how the body works. Stone pine is an important nut-producing tree. Its cones are harvested from natural forests and planted groups of trees in Spain, Portugal, Italy, Turkey, and Tunisia. People have eaten pine nuts since ancient times. Researchers are now selecting trees with useful inherited features and developing ways to care for them so that stone pine can be grown more successfully as a farm crop.

## introduction_rest

People were already planting stone pine outside its natural home regions about 2,800 years ago. More recently, the chance to sell pine nuts has encouraged growers in Argentina, Australia, New Zealand, and Chile to plant these trees. Growers and researchers are looking for ways to increase the amount of edible nuts harvested. This matters because edible nuts make up only a small part of a cone’s total weight. The harvest can change greatly from one year to the next. It depends on how many cones the trees produce, as well as the size, weight, and number of seeds and nuts inside them.

### Paragraph 3

In recent decades, researchers in Europe have reported a sharp drop in the amount of seeds and edible nuts obtained from pine cones. Seeds still in their shells once made up about 17% of a cone’s weight, but that figure has fallen to about 5%. The edible nuts inside those shells have fallen from about 4% of cone weight to 2% or less. Even cones that look healthy can contain seeds with nothing inside: in some cases, up to half the seeds are empty. This matters because companies buy cones by their weight, not by how much food they contain. Researchers have linked empty and damaged seeds to long dry periods and to a seed-feeding insect called *Leptoglossus occidentalis*. This insect was first detected in Chile in 2017 and in Argentina in 2019. Earlier studies found that heavier cones tended to contain more seeds and heavier nuts. However, one study in Chile did not find clear evidence that edible nuts made up a larger share of the weight of heavier cones.

### Paragraph 4

Earlier research has linked bigger cones with better-quality harvests. Watering trees and adding nutrients to the soil might help them grow heavier cones and provide more edible nuts. In this study, we compared heavy and light cones collected in different places in Chile over several years. We measured the share of cone weight made up of edible nuts and checked how many seeds were damaged. We expected heavy cones to provide a larger share of edible nuts and to have a smaller share of damaged seeds. Knowing whether this is true could help growers improve pine-nut production in Chile. Comparing our results with findings from other countries could also help growers and businesses elsewhere.

## methods

### Where we collected the cones

We studied seven plantations of adult stone pine trees in Chile, between the Valparaíso and Araucanía regions. Table 1 gives each plantation’s location and its average weather conditions from 2010 to 2020. Table 2 describes the plantations, including the trees’ ages, sizes, and spacing.

### How we collected and measured them

We collected cones during winter from 2010 to 2020, except in 2016. At each plantation, we randomly selected ten trees and then randomly selected one healthy-looking, three-year-old cone from each tree. Our aim was to collect ten cones per plantation each year. Sometimes, difficulties with harvesting meant we collected fewer. We weighed each cone immediately after picking it.

Over the whole study, we collected 560 cones. Each year, we took the cones to INFOR’s laboratory and removed their seeds. Here, we use **“seed”** to mean the seed with its shell still on, and **“pine nut”** to mean the edible part inside that shell.

We counted the seeds in each cone and measured the size and weight of the seeds and pine nuts. We also counted empty and damaged seeds. For the samples collected from 2010 to 2015, we measured all the seeds and healthy pine nuts, but only combined measurements are available for that period, rather than separate records for every item. From 2017 to 2020, we counted and weighed the seeds and pine nuts in each cone and randomly selected a sample of 20 for more detailed measurements.

### What we measured

Table 3 describes the measurements and equipment. We recorded:

- **Cone weight:** how much each freshly picked cone weighed.
- **Cone size:** its length and its width at the widest point.
- **Seed number:** how many seeds, still in their shells, each cone contained.
- **Seed size and weight:** the length, width, and weight of the seeds.
- **Pine nut number:** how many healthy, edible nuts each cone contained.
- **Pine nut size and weight:** the length, width, and weight of the edible nuts.
- **Empty or damaged seeds:** how many seeds were empty or damaged.

We used a Mettler AJ150 balance to measure weight. To measure length and width, we used a digital caliper—a tool with movable jaws that fit around an object. We recorded weights in grams and lengths in millimetres.

Before weighing the edible nuts, we dried them in a Red Line Binder oven at 40°C until their moisture level was 6%.

We calculated three percentages to describe how much of the harvested material could become food:

1. **Seeds as a share of cone weight:** divide the total weight of seeds, including their shells, by the cone’s weight, then multiply by 100.
2. **Edible nuts as a share of seed weight:** divide the total weight of edible nuts by the total weight of seeds with their shells, then multiply by 100.
3. **Edible nuts as a share of cone weight:** divide the total weight of edible nuts by the cone’s weight, then multiply by 100.

The third percentage was our main measure of how much food a cone provided. For example, a result of 4% means that every 100 grams of cone provided 4 grams of edible nuts.

### How we compared heavy and light cones

We put all 560 cones in order from lightest to heaviest, using cones from every plantation and collection year. We then divided them into three roughly equal groups. We used the lightest third as our **light-cone group** and the heaviest third as our **heavy-cone group**. The middle third was not part of this comparison.

We compared the two groups’ average measurements, including seed and nut size, the percentage of damaged seeds, and the percentage of cone weight made up of edible nuts.

For this comparison, we used a mathematical method called a **mixed model**. It helped us separate differences linked to cone weight from differences linked to where and when the cones were collected. We compared the two weight groups and the seven particular plantations directly. We also allowed for variation between years and for the ways these factors could work together—for example, the difference between heavy and light cones might change from one year to another.

We used a statistical cutoff of 0.05 when checking for differences. This means we counted a result as evidence of a difference if a model assuming no real difference would produce a result at least this extreme less than 5% of the time, provided the model’s assumptions were met. It does **not** mean there was a 95% chance that our explanation was correct.

Measurements might also be more spread out in one weight group than in the other. We therefore tried two versions of the model: one that assumed the same amount of spread in both groups, and one that allowed different amounts. We compared them using a score called **AIC**, which balances how well a model describes the measurements against how complicated it is. This score favoured the version that allowed different amounts of spread.

### How we looked for other patterns

We also used a method called a **regression tree** to look for features linked to the percentage of cone weight made up of edible nuts. This method repeatedly splits the cones into groups using questions such as whether a cone contains more or fewer nuts than a chosen number. These splits help show which measured features are useful for explaining differences between cones. They do not, by themselves, prove that one feature causes another.

We used charts called **box plots** to show the middle values and the spread of important measurements at each plantation. We carried out the calculations using the InfoStat software and its connection to the R software.

## results

### How heavy and light cones differed

Across the plantations and years, cones weighed about 470 grams on average. Edible pine nuts made up an average of 3.91% of cone weight—about 3.91 grams of food for every 100 grams of cone.

The light-cone group contained cones weighing less than 393 grams. The heavy-cone group contained cones weighing more than 503 grams.

We did not find clear evidence that the differences between heavy and light cones changed from one plantation to another. This does not prove that the pattern was exactly the same everywhere, but our tests did not detect a clear difference between sites.

### Seeds inside their shells

Heavy cones contained about 125 seeds each, compared with 89 in light cones—a difference of 40.0%.

The seeds in heavy cones were also larger. On average, they were:

- **26.3% heavier**
- **13.3% longer**
- **9.5% wider**

Seeds, including their shells, also made up a larger share of the heavy cones’ weight. That share was 5.8% higher than in light cones.

### Edible pine nuts

Heavy cones contained about 113 healthy, edible pine nuts each, compared with 76 in light cones. This was a difference of 48.4%.

The edible nuts from heavy cones were, on average:

- **22.2% heavier**
- **13.0% longer**
- **4.1% wider**

Edible nuts made up **4.05% of the weight of heavy cones**, compared with **3.62% for light cones**. In other words, the same weight of heavy cones provided 11.9% more edible nuts by weight than light cones.

Heavy cones also had a smaller share of empty or damaged seeds: **9.0%, compared with 15.9%** in light cones.

Table 4 gives the full measurements and shows the uncertainty around the estimated averages.

### Which features were linked to more edible nuts?

Our branching comparison, shown in Figure 1, first separated cones by the number of healthy pine nuts they contained.

For cones with **78 or fewer nuts**, edible nuts made up an average of **2.60% of cone weight**. For cones with **79 or more**, the average was **4.04%**. The first group therefore provided about 35.6% less edible nut weight for the same weight of cones.

Among cones with fewer nuts, there was another clear split:

- Cones with **45 or fewer nuts** provided an average of **1.83%** of their weight as edible nuts.
- Cones with **46 to 78 nuts** provided an average of **3.02%**.

The first of these groups had a 39.4% lower share of edible nuts than the second.

For cones containing **79 or more nuts**, the amount of edible nut inside the seed shells also mattered. Where edible nuts made up more than 21.0% of the seeds’ total weight, including shells, they accounted for an average of **4.54% of cone weight**. Where that share was 21.0% or less, the average was **3.64%**.

Within the group above 21.0%, there was one more useful split. Where seeds with their shells made up more than 20.8% of cone weight, edible nuts accounted for **5.10% of cone weight**. Where seeds made up 20.8% or less, the figure was **4.35%**.

These comparisons show which features went together with a greater share of edible nuts. They do not, on their own, show what would happen if a grower changed how the trees were managed.

## discussion

### Why cone weight matters

Size is often an important sign of quality in fruit crops. Earlier studies of stone pine found that larger cones tended to be heavier, and heavier cones tended to contain heavier seeds and edible nuts. Weather also matters. Rainfall during the spring of a cone’s final year of growth can affect its weight. This helps explain why cone weight can change greatly from one year to another. In Chile, more rainfall and higher average temperatures have also been linked to heavier cones.

### How the Chilean harvest compares with other countries

The amount of edible nuts obtained from a cone depends partly on how many nuts it contains. However, pests and diseases can damage the seeds, making cone weight alone a less reliable guide to how much food is inside.

The cones in our study weighed about 470 grams on average, more than reported in places where stone pine grows naturally. Once the seeds were removed from the cones, their edible contents made up an average of 20.3% of the seeds’ weight, including shells. This was similar to results from the tree’s natural home regions.

Edible nuts made up about 3.9% of the whole cone’s weight. That was also close to earlier figures from Italy, at 3.6%, and Spain, at 2.7–4.4%.

However, our figure was higher than several European figures reported after the arrival of the seed-feeding insect *Leptoglossus occidentalis*. Following its arrival, reported shares of edible nuts fell sharply: studies recorded 1.1–2.1% of cone weight in Spain, and 1.7% and 3.0% in Portugal. Other studies have reported a falling share of edible nuts in Chile too.

### What heavier cones provided

Across the places and years we studied, edible nuts made up an 11.9% larger share of the weight of heavy cones than of light cones. This agrees with some earlier research. However, it differs from another study in the tree’s natural home region, which did not find a clear link between cone weight and the share of edible nuts.

The advantage of heavy cones came with two important differences: they contained 48.4% more healthy pine nuts, and their individual nuts were 22.2% heavier. Having more nuts and heavier nuts helps explain why these cones provided more food.

Our cones also contained more edible nuts than reported in studies from Turkey, Italy, and Portugal. Other crops have shown a similar pattern: heavier fruits containing several seeds can provide a larger share of useful food.

### Seed damage remains a concern

Heavy cones had a smaller share of damaged seeds than light cones, in line with earlier findings. This also helped them provide more edible nuts.

Across our samples, an average of 12.8% of seeds were damaged. That was similar to a figure reported in Croatia and lower than figures from several other countries:

- **Tunisia:** 19.3%
- **Spain:** 50% and 60% in separate studies
- **Lebanon:** 60%

However, our average was about twice the figure reported in a Chilean study published in 2018. Damage from *Leptoglossus occidentalis* could help explain this difference. Another possible explanation is the severe, long-lasting drought affecting Chile since 2010, with rainfall shortages reaching 40%. Our results do not establish how much either factor contributed.

We also compared three nearby plantations—Quilvo, Cáhuil, and Rosario—which receive similar rainfall. Quilvo received extra watering. However, the differences we observed were not enough to show that this watering caused a better harvest.

### What growers could take from these findings

Cone weight varies greatly even within a single plantation. The differences between heavy and light cones therefore matter when judging a harvest.

Our findings suggest that weight can be a useful clue to cone quality. Heavier cones tended to contain more seeds, larger edible nuts, and a greater share of edible nut weight. This agrees with some earlier studies. Weight is a useful guide, though—not a guarantee of what is inside every cone.

### What should be tested next

By following several plantations over many years, we found a link between cone weight and the share of edible nuts obtained. This suggests that growing heavier cones could help improve harvests.

Earlier studies provide reasons to investigate this idea. Some found that adding nutrients to the soil increased cone weight or the share of edible nuts. Benefits have been reported for nutrients trees need in large amounts and for those they need only in small amounts. Other studies found that watering increased cone weight.

The next step is to test how these practices affect both cone weight and the amount of edible nuts harvested. This would help establish which changes actually improve production and whether they are worth the cost to growers.

## conclusion

The stone pine cones we studied in Chile were heavier than those reported in the tree’s natural home regions. The amount of edible nuts obtained depended on the size, weight, and health of the seeds and nuts. Heavy cones contained 48.4% more healthy pine nuts than light cones. Edible nuts also made up a larger share of their weight—an increase of 11.9% compared with light cones. We therefore recommend exploring ways to grow heavier cones, such as watering trees and adding nutrients to the soil, to increase the amount of edible nuts harvested.
