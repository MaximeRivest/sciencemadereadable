Explaining terms
We did all the math using a computer program called R (version 4.3.0).
• Statistical Analysis: This means figuring out if what we saw was real or just luck. We used a method called 'Linear mixed-effects models'. A simple way to understand this is that we looked at many different pieces of data (like different years and different places).
• Treatment Level: Because we wanted to see the effect of the policy (fencing, control, etc.) on the research (plants), we treated the treatment and the environment as 'fixed effects'. This means we were testing these specific things.
• Variation: We also had to account for places (counties). We treated them as 'random effects'. This means we thought of counties as just one big group to help us make the math work better across all the data.

We analyzed how the policy and the weather changed three types of grass (avoidance, non-palatable, and palatable). We did this for a lot of observations. We had 1,460 data points, representing all the measurements we took in all the plots from the beginning to the end of the experiment.

When we compared results, we looked for something called 'statistically significant'. This means that a difference we saw is very unlikely to happen just by luck. A result is considered statistically significant if the p-value is less than 0.05. In our drawings, the average value is shown with the margin of error (calculated as mean ± SEM), and the trend over time is shown with a 95% confidence interval.

Structural equation modeling (SEM)
To understand the causes of the changes we saw, we used a method called Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). This method helps us figure out the causes of a problem. We used a package piecewiseSEM on this. We also used dplyr to help organize the data, and BioRender to make the pictures.

We did the statistical analyses in R (version 4.3.0). We used linear mixed-effects models, which let us test how a measurement changes with several factors while allowing for differences between groups of plots. We ran them with the ‘lmer’ function in the ‘lme4’ package (version 1.1.33). We tested how climate and management affected the number of plant species, the stability of the grassland and the amount of plant material above the ground, separately for the three groups of grasses: those livestock avoid, those they do not eat and those they eat.

In these models, the management measures (control, fencing and plateau pika control), the climate measures (average yearly temperature and yearly rainfall) and their interactions were treated as fixed effects. An interaction means we checked whether the effect of one factor depended on the other. We treated ‘county’ as a random effect, which allows each county to have its own starting level without having to estimate a separate effect for every county.

All our statistical models used 1,460 observations in total. These were the yearly measurements taken across all study plots during the whole experimental period. In all results from the linear mixed-effects models, asterisks (∗∗∗) mark statistical significance at p < 0.05. This means that if there were really no difference, a gap this large would appear by chance less than 5% of the time. In all graphs, the summary plots show the mean ± SEM, where SEM is the standard error of the mean, a measure of how precisely the mean is known. The plots showing changes over time include 95% confidence intervals, which show the range in which the true value is likely to lie.

We did our statistical work in R (version 4.3.0). Our main tool was a type of model called a linear mixed-effects model, built with the 'lmer' function from the 'lme4' package (version 1.1.33). We used these models to measure how climate and land management affected three things: the number of plant species, how stable the grassland was, and the amount of plant material. We looked at each of these separately for the three groups of plants: those livestock avoid, those they don't like to eat, and those they like to eat.

A mixed-effects model sorts the possible influences into two kinds. The first kind, called fixed effects, are the influences we actually wanted to measure. Ours were the type of management (control, fencing or plateau pika control), the climate (the average yearly temperature and the yearly rain and snow), and their interactions. An interaction asks whether the effect of one factor changes depending on another, for example whether fencing works differently in wetter years. The second kind, called a random effect, handles background differences we wanted to allow for but not study. We treated "county" this way, because sites in the same county may share local conditions.

Every model used a total of 1,460 observations. These add up all the yearly measurements taken across all the study plots over the whole experiment. In the results from these models, we mark statistically significant findings with asterisks (∗∗∗), standing for p < 0.05. A result is called statistically significant when, if there were really no difference, a gap this large would appear by chance alone less than 5% of the time.

In our graphs, the summary plots show the average plus or minus the standard error of the mean. The standard error shows roughly how precisely we have pinned down the average. The graphs of changes over time also show 95% confidence intervals, a shaded range that shows how uncertain each estimate is.

To understand what drives changes in how stable the grassland is, we used structural equation modeling. We ran it with the 'psem' function from the 'piecewiseSEM' package (version 2.3.0). We used the 'dplyr' package (version 1.1.2) to organize the data. Most of our pictures and diagrams were made with BioRender.com.
