Statistical analyses First, cone weight categories were defined as the extreme tertiles of cone weight probabilistic distribution using all harvested cones across plantations and years (n = 560). The ANOVA mixed model was fitted with homogeneous and heterogeneous variances for each cone weight category. The Akaike information criteria (AIC) was used to select the best model (the heteroscedastic model). ### Where we collected the cones We studied seven plantations of adult stone pine trees in Chile, between the Valparaíso and Araucanía regions. Table 1 gives each plantation’s location and its average weather conditions from 2010 to 2020. Table 2 describes the plantations, including the trees’ ages, sizes, and spacing. ### How we collected and measured them We collected cones during winter from 2010 to 2020, except in 2016. At each plantation, we randomly selected ten trees and then randomly selected one healthy-looking, three-year-old cone from each tree. Our aim was to collect ten cones per plantation each year. Sometimes, difficulties with harvesting meant we collected fewer. We weighed each cone immediately after picking it. Over the whole study, we collected 560 cones. Each year, we took the cones to INFOR’s laboratory and removed their seeds. Here, we use **“seed”** to mean the seed with its shell still on, and **“pine nut”** to mean the edible part inside that shell. We counted the seeds in each cone and measured the size and weight of the seeds and pine nuts. We also counted empty and damaged seeds. For the samples collected from 2010 to 2015, we measured all the seeds and healthy pine nuts, but only combined measurements are available for that period, rather than separate records for every item. From 2017 to 2020, we counted and weighed the seeds and pine nuts in each cone and randomly selected a sample of 20 for more detailed measurements. ### What we measured Table 3 describes the measurements and equipment. We recorded: - **Cone weight:** how much each freshly picked cone weighed. - **Cone size:** its length and its width at the widest point. - **Seed number:** how many seeds, still in their shells, each cone contained. - **Seed size and weight:** the length, width, and weight of the seeds. - **Pine nut number:** how many healthy, edible nuts each cone contained. - **Pine nut size and weight:** the length, width, and weight of the edible nuts. - **Empty or damaged seeds:** how many seeds were empty or damaged. We used a Mettler AJ150 balance to measure weight. To measure length and width, we used a digital caliper—a tool with movable jaws that fit around an object. We recorded weights in grams and lengths in millimetres. Before weighing the edible nuts, we dried them in a Red Line Binder oven at 40°C until their moisture level was 6%. We calculated three percentages to describe how much of the harvested material could become food: 1. **Seeds as a share of cone weight:** divide the total weight of seeds, including their shells, by the cone’s weight, then multiply by 100. 2. **Edible nuts as a share of seed weight:** divide the total weight of edible nuts by the total weight of seeds with their shells, then multiply by 100. 3. **Edible nuts as a share of cone weight:** divide the total weight of edible nuts by the cone’s weight, then multiply by 100. The third percentage was our main measure of how much food a cone provided. For example, a result of 4% means that every 100 grams of cone provided 4 grams of edible nuts. ### How we compared heavy and light cones We put all 560 cones in order from lightest to heaviest, using cones from every plantation and collection year. We then divided them into three roughly equal groups. We used the lightest third as our **light-cone group** and the heaviest third as our **heavy-cone group**. The middle third was not part of this comparison. We compared the two groups’ average measurements, including seed and nut size, the percentage of damaged seeds, and the percentage of cone weight made up of edible nuts. For this comparison, we used a mathematical method called a **mixed model**. It helped us separate differences linked to cone weight from differences linked to where and when the cones were collected. We compared the two weight groups and the seven particular plantations directly. We also allowed for variation between years and for the ways these factors could work together—for example, the difference between heavy and light cones might change from one year to another. We used a statistical cutoff of 0.05 when checking for differences. This means we counted a result as evidence of a difference if a model assuming no real difference would produce a result at least this extreme less than 5% of the time, provided the model’s assumptions were met. It does **not** mean there was a 95% chance that our explanation was correct. Measurements might also be more spread out in one weight group than in the other. We therefore tried two versions of the model: one that assumed the same amount of spread in both groups, and one that allowed different amounts. We compared them using a score called **AIC**, which balances how well a model describes the measurements against how complicated it is. This score favoured the version that allowed different amounts of spread. ### How we looked for other patterns We also used a method called a **regression tree** to look for features linked to the percentage of cone weight made up of edible nuts. This method repeatedly splits the cones into groups using questions such as whether a cone contains more or fewer nuts than a chosen number. These splits help show which measured features are useful for explaining differences between cones. They do not, by themselves, prove that one feature causes another. We used charts called **box plots** to show the middle values and the spread of important measurements at each plantation. We carried out the calculations using the InfoStat software and its connection to the R software.

## Materials and methods ### Plantations and cone sampling We sampled cones from seven mature stone pine plantations in Chile, between the Valparaíso and Araucanía regions. The sites were Santo Domingo, Rosario, Cáhuil, Quilvo, Toconey, Mulchén and Antiquina. Table 1 gives their exact locations, elevations in metres above sea level, and climate averages for 2010–2020. The climate measures include annual average temperature and autumn maximum temperature in degrees Celsius (°C), and annual rainfall in millimetres (mm). Autumn here runs from 21 March to 20 June. Earlier research found evidence of a link between autumn maximum temperature and the number of seeds per cone [9]. We obtained climate data from the Chilean National Environmental Information System (www.dga.cl; www.inia.cl). Quilvo also received irrigation in spring and summer (Table 1). Table 2 describes the plantations in 2020. It lists tree age in years, trunk diameter at chest height (DBH) in centimetres (cm), tree height in metres (m), plantation area, and tree spacing in metres × metres. We sampled these plantations each winter from 2010 through 2020, except in 2016. Our target was 10 cones per plantation each year. We used **hierarchical random sampling**, meaning random selection in steps: first we selected 10 trees within each plantation, then one healthy-looking, three-year-old cone from each selected tree. Harvesting difficulties meant that some plantation samples contained fewer than 10 cones in some years. Altogether, we collected 560 cones. We recorded each cone’s fresh weight immediately after harvest [24, 25]. ### Laboratory measurements Each year, we processed the harvested cones at INFOR’s laboratory. We extracted and counted all the seeds from each cone, removed their shells, and counted the healthy edible kernels. We also recorded empty and damaged seeds to monitor seed health. Table 3 lists the measurements, their units, and the procedures used. We measured cone, seed and kernel weights in grams (g), using a Mettler AJ150 balance. Lengths and diameters were measured in millimetres with a digital caliper, a tool for measuring the size of small objects. Diameters were measured across the widest part. Before weighing the kernels, we dried them at 40°C to a water content of 6%, using a Red Line Binder oven. Table 3 retains the equipment details. The measurements and available data differed between two periods: - **2010–2015:** We measured all seeds in their shells and all healthy kernels from each cone. Only combined, or aggregated, measurement data are available for this period. - **2017–2020:** We counted and weighed all seeds and kernels from each cone, while some individual seed and kernel measurements used random samples of 20 per cone. Table 3 identifies which measurements used these smaller samples [26]. ### Weight-based yields We calculated three weight percentages for each cone, using the formulas in Table 3. Each percentage compares the weight of material obtained with the weight of the starting material: - **Seed-with-shell share of cone weight (cone-to-seed yield, SY)** = (total weight of seeds in their shells ÷ fresh cone weight) × 100. - **Healthy-kernel share of seed-with-shell weight (seed-to-pine-nut yield, SPY)** = (total weight of healthy dried kernels ÷ total weight of seeds in their shells) × 100. - **Edible share (cone-to-pine-nut yield, PY)** = (total weight of healthy dried kernels ÷ fresh cone weight) × 100. All weights in each calculation refer to the same cone and are measured in grams. For the weight amounts at the top of these fractions, Table 3 uses seed count × seed weight, or healthy-kernel count × kernel weight. Multiplying an item count by the average weight per item gives a total weight. The edible share compares kernels dried to 6% water content with cones weighed fresh at harvest. For example, an edible share of 4% means 4 g of these dried healthy kernels for every 100 g of fresh cone. It is a percentage by weight, not the percentage of seeds that are healthy or the total harvest from a tree. ### Statistical analysis We used the weight distribution of all 560 cones, pooled across plantations and years, to define two contrasting groups. Light cones came from the lightest third of the weight distribution, and heavy cones from the heaviest third. These two end groups are called the **extreme tertiles**. The middle third was left out of the light–heavy comparison. We compared the groups’ average cone, seed and kernel traits, such as their sizes and weights, along with their percentage of empty or damaged seeds and their edible share. We used a **mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA)** across plantations and years [27]. This method compares group averages while accounting for other sources of variation in the data. Cone-weight group and plantation were treated as **fixed effects**: the model estimated differences between these particular groups and sites. Year and the corresponding **interactions** were treated as **random effects**, representing sources of variation that the model allowed for. An interaction means that a pattern linked to one factor, such as cone-weight group, can change depending on another factor, such as plantation or year. We used a statistical significance threshold of α = 0.05. Under the model’s assumptions, a single test using this threshold would falsely signal a difference about 5 times in 100 repeated studies if no real difference existed. This threshold is not the probability that our prediction is correct. We fitted two versions of the mixed model. One assumed that the remaining variation—the spread not explained by the model—was the same in the light and heavy groups. The other allowed this spread to differ between groups. These choices are called homogeneous and heterogeneous variances. We compared them using the **Akaike information criterion (AIC)**, a score that balances how well a model fits the data against its complexity. Lower scores are preferred. This comparison selected the model allowing different spreads, called the heteroscedastic model. We also used a **regression tree** to examine how cone, seed and kernel traits were linked to differences in edible share [28]. This method repeatedly divides observations into smaller groups using cutoffs in the measured traits. It chooses divisions that make edible shares more similar within each group and separate groups with different average edible shares. The tree shown in Figure 1 contains 328 cones. Its branches describe patterns in the data, not proof that a trait causes a change in edible share. We used **box plots**, graphs showing the middle value and spread of measurements, to describe the main traits linked to edible share for each plantation [27]. The plots in Figure 2 cover 2017–2020. We performed the statistical analyses in InfoStat [29], using its connection to R (www.r-project.org).
