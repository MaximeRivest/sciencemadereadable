Heavier stone pine cones in Chile had a larger share of edible nuts ## Abstract Stone pine (*Pinus pinea*) produces seeds with hard shells. The edible part inside is the pine nut, or kernel. Cone-to-pine-nut yield (PY) is the weight of healthy kernels as a percentage of fresh cone weight. We call this the **edible share**. Kernels were dried to 6% water content before weighing, while cones were weighed fresh at harvest. An edible share of 4% therefore means 4 g of dried kernels for every 100 g of fresh cone. Declines in this share have been reported in Europe and Chile, which matters because pine nuts are in strong demand. We studied 560 healthy-looking, three-year-old cones from seven plantations in Chile, outside the species’ natural range. Sampling covered 10 years between 2010 and 2020; we did not sample in 2016. We counted seeds and healthy kernels, measured their sizes and weights, and recorded empty or damaged seeds. Only combined measurement data were available for 2010–2015. In 2017–2020, some individual measurements used random samples of 20 seeds or kernels per cone (Table 3). We compared the lightest third of cones, weighing less than 393 g, with the heaviest third, weighing more than 503 g. The middle third was not included in this comparison. We used a mixed linear model—a statistical method that allowed us to compare the groups while accounting for differences among plantations and years. Cones averaged about 474 g, compared with about 300 g reported for the species’ natural range. The average edible share across the study was 3.91%. Heavy cones averaged 124.8 seeds in their shells and 112.9 healthy kernels per cone; light cones averaged 89.1 seeds and 76.1 healthy kernels. Seeds and kernels were also larger and heavier in the heavy-cone group (Table 4). The edible share was 4.05% in heavy cones and 3.62% in light cones. This difference of 0.43 percentage points was an 11.9% relative increase from the light-cone value. Empty or damaged seeds accounted for a reported 9.0% of seeds in heavy cones and 15.9% in light cones. Statistical tests supported the heavy group’s higher healthy-kernel count, higher edible share and lower percentage of empty or damaged seeds. We also used a regression tree, which repeatedly splits observations into groups to identify measurements that best separate high and low edible shares. This analysis included 328 cones. Cones with 78 or fewer healthy kernels averaged a 2.60% edible share; those with 79 or more averaged 4.04% (Figure 1). Further divisions involved two weight fractions: how much of a cone consisted of seeds in their shells, and how much of that seed weight was healthy kernel. These describe two steps in obtaining edible nuts: separating seeds from cones, then removing shells and keeping healthy kernels. Heavier cones were associated with a larger edible share, but this does not prove that making cones heavier will increase that share. Irrigation, or adding water, and fertilization, or adding plant nutrients, are possible ways to encourage heavier cones. Their effects on the edible share need testing; this study did not establish that these treatments caused an improvement. ## Introduction Stone pine (*Pinus pinea*) is valued for its edible pine nuts—the kernels inside its hard-shelled seeds. These nuts contain fats and proteins, which provide energy and materials the body needs, along with vitamins and minerals. They also contain bioactive compounds: substances that can affect processes in the body [1]. Stone pine is an important nut-producing tree. Its nuts are harvested from naturally growing forests and from plantations—areas of planted trees—in Spain, Portugal, Italy, Turkey and Tunisia [2]. People have eaten these nuts since ancient times [3]. To develop stone pine as a farm crop, researchers have begun selecting trees with useful inherited traits, meaning features that can pass from parent trees to their offspring. They are also developing better ways to grow and care for the trees [4]. These efforts are part of domestication: making a species better suited to farming through selection and growing methods. People planted stone pine outside its natural range—the places where the species grows naturally—as far back as 2,800 years ago [5]. Opportunities to sell pine nuts have helped expand stone pine farming to countries outside that range, including Argentina [6], Australia [7], New Zealand [8] and Chile [9]. Major efforts are being made to increase pine nut production [10]. This matters because edible kernels make up only a very small part of a cone’s weight [11]. The amount of pine nuts harvested varies greatly from year to year [12]. It is linked to the number of cones and to measurements such as the sizes and weights of cones, seeds and kernels. These features help determine cone-to-pine-nut yield (PY), which we call the **edible share**. This expresses healthy kernel weight as a percentage of cone weight. In our study, kernels were weighed after drying, while cones were weighed fresh at harvest. Reports from Europe describe steep declines in two measures of yield in recent decades. Cone-to-seed yield is the weight of seeds, including their shells, as a percentage of cone weight. This fell from 17% to 5%, while the edible share fell from 4% to 2% or even less. Even healthy-looking cones could contain up to 50% empty seeds—seeds with no developed kernel inside [13]. Businesses buy cones by weight, not by their edible nut content [14]. Tracking the edible share has therefore become more important as empty and damaged seeds have become more common [15]. These seed problems have been linked to the insect *Leptoglossus occidentalis* [16, 17] and to drought [13, 17]. This insect was detected in Chile in 2017 [18, 19] and in Argentina in 2019 [20]. Earlier studies found links between cone weight and the number and weight of pine nuts [21, 22]. However, a study in Chile did not find clear statistical evidence of a link between cone weight and the edible share [23]. That result left the relationship uncertain, rather than proving there was no link. Larger cones have also been linked to better harvest quality [21]. Fertilization (adding plant nutrients) and irrigation (adding water) could encourage trees to produce heavier cones. These practices might also increase the edible share, but that possible benefit needs testing. We aimed to compare the number of damaged seeds and the edible share between heavy and light stone pine cones across a wide range of places and years in Chile. Our prediction was that heavy cones would have a larger edible share and a lower percentage of damaged seeds than light cones. Understanding the edible share is important for expanding stone pine farming in Chile. Comparing harvest measurements with values reported for stone pine in other parts of the world is also useful to the international pine nut industry and related organizations. ## Methods ## Materials and methods ### Plantations and cone sampling We sampled cones from seven mature stone pine plantations in Chile, between the Valparaíso and Araucanía regions. The sites were Santo Domingo, Rosario, Cáhuil, Quilvo, Toconey, Mulchén and Antiquina. Table 1 gives their exact locations, elevations in metres above sea level, and climate averages for 2010–2020. The climate measures include annual average temperature and autumn maximum temperature in degrees Celsius (°C), and annual rainfall in millimetres (mm). Autumn here runs from 21 March to 20 June. Earlier research found evidence of a link between autumn maximum temperature and the number of seeds per cone [9]. We obtained climate data from the Chilean National Environmental Information System (www.dga.cl; www.inia.cl). Quilvo also received irrigation in spring and summer (Table 1). Table 2 describes the plantations in 2020. It lists tree age in years, trunk diameter at chest height (DBH) in centimetres (cm), tree height in metres (m), plantation area, and tree spacing in metres × metres. We sampled these plantations each winter from 2010 through 2020, except in 2016. Our target was 10 cones per plantation each year. We used **hierarchical random sampling**, meaning random selection in steps: first we selected 10 trees within each plantation, then one healthy-looking, three-year-old cone from each selected tree. Harvesting difficulties meant that some plantation samples contained fewer than 10 cones in some years. Altogether, we collected 560 cones. We recorded each cone’s fresh weight immediately after harvest [24, 25]. ### Laboratory measurements Each year, we processed the harvested cones at INFOR’s laboratory. We extracted and counted all the seeds from each cone, removed their shells, and counted the healthy edible kernels. We also recorded empty and damaged seeds to monitor seed health. Table 3 lists the measurements, their units, and the procedures used. We measured cone, seed and kernel weights in grams (g), using a Mettler AJ150 balance. Lengths and diameters were measured in millimetres with a digital caliper, a tool for measuring the size of small objects. Diameters were measured across the widest part. Before weighing the kernels, we dried them at 40°C to a water content of 6%, using a Red Line Binder oven. Table 3 retains the equipment details. The measurements and available data differed between two periods: - **2010–2015:** We measured all seeds in their shells and all healthy kernels from each cone. Only combined, or aggregated, measurement data are available for this period. - **2017–2020:** We counted and weighed all seeds and kernels from each cone, while some individual seed and kernel measurements used random samples of 20 per cone. Table 3 identifies which measurements used these smaller samples [26]. ### Weight-based yields We calculated three weight percentages for each cone, using the formulas in Table 3. Each percentage compares the weight of material obtained with the weight of the starting material: - **Seed-with-shell share of cone weight (cone-to-seed yield, SY)** = (total weight of seeds in their shells ÷ fresh cone weight) × 100. - **Healthy-kernel share of seed-with-shell weight (seed-to-pine-nut yield, SPY)** = (total weight of healthy dried kernels ÷ total weight of seeds in their shells) × 100. - **Edible share (cone-to-pine-nut yield, PY)** = (total weight of healthy dried kernels ÷ fresh cone weight) × 100. All weights in each calculation refer to the same cone and are measured in grams. For the weight amounts at the top of these fractions, Table 3 uses seed count × seed weight, or healthy-kernel count × kernel weight. Multiplying an item count by the average weight per item gives a total weight. The edible share compares kernels dried to 6% water content with cones weighed fresh at harvest. For example, an edible share of 4% means 4 g of these dried healthy kernels for every 100 g of fresh cone. It is a percentage by weight, not the percentage of seeds that are healthy or the total harvest from a tree. ### Statistical analysis We used the weight distribution of all 560 cones, pooled across plantations and years, to define two contrasting groups. Light cones came from the lightest third of the weight distribution, and heavy cones from the heaviest third. These two end groups are called the **extreme tertiles**. The middle third was left out of the light–heavy comparison. We compared the groups’ average cone, seed and kernel traits, such as their sizes and weights, along with their percentage of empty or damaged seeds and their edible share. We used a **mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA)** across plantations and years [27]. This method compares group averages while accounting for other sources of variation in the data. Cone-weight group and plantation were treated as **fixed effects**: the model estimated differences between these particular groups and sites. Year and the corresponding **interactions** were treated as **random effects**, representing sources of variation that the model allowed for. An interaction means that a pattern linked to one factor, such as cone-weight group, can change depending on another factor, such as plantation or year. We used a statistical significance threshold of α = 0.05. Under the model’s assumptions, a single test using this threshold would falsely signal a difference about 5 times in 100 repeated studies if no real difference existed. This threshold is not the probability that our prediction is correct. We fitted two versions of the mixed model. One assumed that the remaining variation—the spread not explained by the model—was the same in the light and heavy groups. The other allowed this spread to differ between groups. These choices are called homogeneous and heterogeneous variances. We compared them using the **Akaike information criterion (AIC)**, a score that balances how well a model fits the data against its complexity. Lower scores are preferred. This comparison selected the model allowing different spreads, called the heteroscedastic model. We also used a **regression tree** to examine how cone, seed and kernel traits were linked to differences in edible share [28]. This method repeatedly divides observations into smaller groups using cutoffs in the measured traits. It chooses divisions that make edible shares more similar within each group and separate groups with different average edible shares. The tree shown in Figure 1 contains 328 cones. Its branches describe patterns in the data, not proof that a trait causes a change in edible share. We used **box plots**, graphs showing the middle value and spread of measurements, to describe the main traits linked to edible share for each plantation [27]. The plots in Figure 2 cover 2017–2020. We performed the statistical analyses in InfoStat [29], using its connection to R (www.r-project.org). ## Results ## Results ### Comparing heavy and light cones Seeds in these results include their shells. Kernels are the edible parts inside the shells; the kernel counts reported here include only healthy ones. The **edible share (PY)** is the weight of dried healthy kernels as a percentage of fresh cone weight. For the results from Table 4, the number after **±** is the **standard error**. It describes uncertainty in an estimated average, not the full spread of individual measurements. For a percentage, the standard error is in percentage points. Across plantations and years, fresh cone weight averaged 473.6 ± 25.8 g, or about 474 g. The average edible share was 3.91 ± 0.16% of fresh cone weight (Table 4). We compared light cones, weighing less than 393 g, with heavy cones, weighing more than 503 g. These groups were the lightest and heaviest thirds of the cone-weight distribution; the middle third was left out of the comparison. Light cones averaged 338.0 ± 4.7 g, and heavy cones averaged 594.9 ± 5.7 g. For every measured trait, the statistical tests found no clear evidence that the heavy–light difference varied among plantations (p > 0.05). This does not prove that the pattern was identical at every plantation. Heavy cones contained more seeds and healthy kernels per cone. Their seeds and kernels were also heavier, longer and wider. The tests supported the differences shown below (Table 4). The figure includes 328 cones from the light and heavy categories—not all 560 cones collected across the study. The first division used healthy-kernel count per cone, with a cutoff of 78.5. Because kernels are counted in whole numbers, this separates cones with 78 or fewer healthy kernels from those with 79 or more: - The 96 cones with **78 or fewer healthy kernels** had an average edible share of **2.60%**. - The 232 cones with **79 or more healthy kernels** had an average edible share of **4.04%**. The lower-count group’s edible share was 35.6% lower relative to the higher-count group’s 4.04% share. Both edible shares express dried healthy-kernel weight as a percentage of fresh cone weight. Within the group with 78 or fewer healthy kernels, the tree made another division at 45.5 kernels. The 34 cones with **45 or fewer kernels** averaged a **1.83%** edible share. The 62 cones with **46–78 kernels** averaged **3.02%**. The 1.83% share was 39.4% lower relative to the 3.02% share in the 46–78-kernel group. Among cones with **79 or more healthy kernels**, the next division used the healthy-kernel share of seed-with-shell weight. Where dried healthy kernels accounted for **more than 21.0% of total seed-with-shell weight**, the average edible share was **4.54%** (104 cones). Where that seed-based share was **21.0% or less**, the average edible share was **3.64%** (128 cones). Earlier studies of stone pine found that heavier cones tended to be larger and to contain heavier seeds and kernels [23, 31]. Other research linked cone weight to spring weather in the final year of cone development, especially rainfall [32, 33]. These weather differences may help explain why cone weight varies greatly from year to year in the species’ natural range [34]. Research in Chile also linked greater annual rainfall and higher average temperatures with heavier cones [9]. Understanding how cone weight relates to edible share has become more important because pests and diseases can damage seeds. The number of healthy kernels matters: it is one part of the edible-share calculation. Across our sites and sampled years in 2010–2020, cones averaged about 474 g. This was higher than weights reported for stone pine’s natural range [34, 36–40]. Healthy dried kernels made up an average of 20.3% of the total weight of seeds in their shells. This seed-based share was similar to values reported from the natural range [36, 41–43]. Our average **edible share** was 3.9% of fresh cone weight. This was close to a historical value of 3.6% for Italy [40, 44] and within the historical range of 2.7–4.4% for Spain [45]. It was higher, however, than European values reported after the arrival of the insect *L. occidentalis*: 1.1–2.1% in Spain [31, 45], and 1.7% [46] and 3.0% [44] in Portugal. European reports have linked large declines in edible share to this insect. Earlier studies have also reported a downward trend in edible share in Chile [23, 47]. These international comparisons use values from separate studies. They are not experiments in which growing conditions were held the same across countries, so they do not establish why the values differ. ### What cone weight may tell us Across plantations and years, the heaviest third of cones had an edible share of 4.05%, compared with 3.62% in the lightest third. The difference was 0.43 percentage points, or an 11.9% relative increase over the light-cone value. This agrees with earlier research [36], but differs from a report from the natural range that found no clear link between cone weight and edible share [32]. As noted in the Introduction, earlier Chilean work also left this link uncertain [23]. The larger edible share in heavy cones went along with 48.4% more healthy kernels per cone and 22.2% greater weight per kernel. These two measurements are key parts of the edible-share calculation and were identified as important in earlier research [23]. Heavy cones also contained more seeds in their shells and had larger kernels (Table 4), matching earlier findings [36]. Our measured number of healthy kernels per cone—also called **cone filling**—was higher than values reported for Turkey [48], Italy [49] and Portugal [22]. More kernels in each cone does not, by itself, establish a larger total harvest per tree or plantation. Similar links between heavier fruits or other seed-holding parts and higher yields have been reported for other crops [50–52]. Cone weights vary greatly within plantations, so the heavy–light difference in edible share could matter in practice. Our findings suggest that cone weight may help indicate cone quality, as earlier work also suggested [36, 61]. ### Seed damage and possible explanations The statistical comparison supported a lower percentage of empty or damaged seeds in heavy cones: 9.0%, compared with 15.9% in light cones (Table 4). This agrees with earlier research [53]. The lower damage percentage occurred alongside the heavy cones’ larger edible share. Across sites and years, the average reported percentage of empty or damaged seeds was 12.8%. This was similar to the percentage reported for Croatia [54] and lower than values reported for Tunisia (19.3% [55]), Spain (50% [56] and 60% [57]) and Lebanon (60% [58]). However, our average damaged-seed count was reported as twice the value in a Chilean study published in 2018 [59]. This difference could reflect damage by *L. occidentalis*, the severe, long-lasting drought that began in central Chile in 2010, or both. During this **mega-drought**, rainfall was reported to be as much as 40% below usual levels [60]. Our study did not establish whether either factor explains the difference in seed damage. ### What to test next The site comparisons limit what we can conclude about irrigation. Quilvo, Cáhuil and Rosario are geographically close and have similar rainfall patterns. Quilvo received added water through irrigation. However, the differences in cone, seed and kernel measurements among these sites (Figure 2) are not enough to show that irrigation caused those differences. Fertilization and irrigation remain possible ways to encourage heavier cones. Earlier studies reported a larger edible share with fertilization [61], as well as greater cone weight [62, 63]. Research on mature stone pine trees has examined adding nutrients needed in small amounts, called **micronutrients** [64, 65], and nutrients needed in larger amounts, called **macronutrients** [21, 38]. Earlier work also reported heavier stone pine cones with irrigation [61, 67]. These are findings from previous studies, not treatment effects established by our measurements. Our observations across several places and years show a link between cone weight and edible share. They do not prove that making cones heavier will increase that share, and we did not run a controlled test of irrigation or fertilization. Further studies should test these and other growing practices to find out whether they increase cone weight and edible share, and whether any gains bring financial benefits for growers. ## Conclusion Our study of seven Chilean plantations found cones averaging about 474 g, compared with about 300 g reported for stone pine’s natural range. The heaviest third of cones, weighing more than 503 g, had larger and heavier seeds and kernels and a smaller percentage of empty or damaged seeds than the lightest third, weighing less than 393 g (Table 4). Heavy cones averaged 112.9 healthy kernels per cone, compared with 76.1 in light cones—a 48.4% higher count. But nut count per cone is different from cone-to-pine-nut yield (PY): the weight of dried healthy kernels as a percentage of fresh cone weight. This edible share was 4.05% in heavy cones and 3.62% in light cones. That is a difference of 0.43 percentage points, or 11.9% more edible kernel weight for the same fresh cone weight. These results describe the nut content of cones, not the total harvest per tree or plantation. Seeds in their shells made up 19.8% of fresh cone weight in heavy cones, compared with 18.7% in light cones. However, healthy kernels made up 20.9% versus 20.3% of seed-with-shell weight, and the statistical test did not establish a clear difference for this separate measure (Table 4). The edible share reflects both how much seed material a cone contains and how much healthy edible material those seeds hold. Cone weight may help assess cone quality, but the observed link does not prove that making cones heavier will raise the edible share. Added water through irrigation or plant nutrients through fertilization could encourage heavier cones. However, our study did not test these practices as controlled treatments. The findings support testing these approaches, rather than treating them as established ways to improve the edible share.
