abstract — original
In Pinus pinea, cone to pine nut yield (total pine nut weight expressed as percentage of cone weight), an important crop trait, is decreasing worldwide. This phenomenon is of great concern, since the nuts of this species are highly demanded. Cone weight, seed and pine nut morphometry, and pine nut yield were monitored in a non-native area in Chile for 10 years. For this purpose, 560 cones, and the seeds and pine nuts contained in them, were counted, measured and weighed in a multi-environment study involving seven plantations. Seed and pine nut damage was evaluated. Two contrasting categories of cone weight (heavy/light) were defined. Cone to pine nut yield (PY) and other traits were calculated and compared between categories using a mixed linear model. Regression trees were used to explain PY variability. Cone weight was higher than in the species’ native range (474 g vs 300 g on average). Pine nut number per cone and PY were significantly higher in the heavy cone category than in the light cone category (125 vs 89 units, and 4.05 vs 3.62%, respectively), The percentage of damaged seeds was lower in heavy than in light cones (9.0% vs 15.9%). Thus, PY depended on seed and pine nut morphometry as well as on seed health. Management practices, such as fertilization and irrigation, could be used to boost production of heavy cones and consequently increase PY.
abstract — astra
Stone pine (*Pinus pinea*) produces seeds with hard shells. The edible part inside is the pine nut, or kernel. Cone-to-pine-nut yield (PY) is the weight of healthy kernels as a percentage of fresh cone weight. We call this the **edible share**. Kernels were dried to 6% water content before weighing, while cones were weighed fresh at harvest. An edible share of 4% therefore means 4 g of dried kernels for every 100 g of fresh cone. Declines in this share have been reported in Europe and Chile, which matters because pine nuts are in strong demand. We studied 560 healthy-looking, three-year-old cones from seven plantations in Chile, outside the species’ natural range. Sampling covered 10 years between 2010 and 2020; we did not sample in 2016. We counted seeds and healthy kernels, measured their sizes and weights, and recorded empty or damaged seeds. Only combined measurement data were available for 2010–2015. In 2017–2020, some individual measurements used random samples of 20 seeds or kernels per cone (Table 3). We compared the lightest third of cones, weighing less than 393 g, with the heaviest third, weighing more than 503 g. The middle third was not included in this comparison. We used a mixed linear model—a statistical method that allowed us to compare the groups while accounting for differences among plantations and years. Cones averaged about 474 g, compared with about 300 g reported for the species’ natural range. The average edible share across the study was 3.91%. Heavy cones averaged 124.8 seeds in their shells and 112.9 healthy kernels per cone; light cones averaged 89.1 seeds and 76.1 healthy kernels. Seeds and kernels were also larger and heavier in the heavy-cone group (Table 4). The edible share was 4.05% in heavy cones and 3.62% in light cones. This difference of 0.43 percentage points was an 11.9% relative increase from the light-cone value. Empty or damaged seeds accounted for a reported 9.0% of seeds in heavy cones and 15.9% in light cones. Statistical tests supported the heavy group’s higher healthy-kernel count, higher edible share and lower percentage of empty or damaged seeds. We also used a regression tree, which repeatedly splits observations into groups to identify measurements that best separate high and low edible shares. This analysis included 328 cones. Cones with 78 or fewer healthy kernels averaged a 2.60% edible share; those with 79 or more averaged 4.04% (Figure 1). Further divisions involved two weight fractions: how much of a cone consisted of seeds in their shells, and how much of that seed weight was healthy kernel. These describe two steps in obtaining edible nuts: separating seeds from cones, then removing shells and keeping healthy kernels. Heavier cones were associated with a larger edible share, but this does not prove that making cones heavier will increase that share. Irrigation, or adding water, and fertilization, or adding plant nutrients, are possible ways to encourage heavier cones. Their effects on the edible share need testing; this study did not establish that these treatments caused an improvement.

Stone pine (*Pinus pinea*) is valued for its edible pine nuts—the kernels inside its hard-shelled seeds. These nuts contain fats and proteins, which provide energy and materials the body needs, along with vitamins and minerals. They also contain bioactive compounds: substances that can affect processes in the body [1]. Stone pine is an important nut-producing tree. Its nuts are harvested from naturally growing forests and from plantations—areas of planted trees—in Spain, Portugal, Italy, Turkey and Tunisia [2]. To develop stone pine as a farm crop, researchers have begun selecting trees with useful inherited traits, meaning features that can pass from parent trees to their offspring. conclusion — astra
Our study of seven Chilean plantations found cones averaging about 474 g, compared with about 300 g reported for stone pine’s natural range. The heaviest third of cones, weighing more than 503 g, had larger and heavier seeds and kernels and a smaller percentage of empty or damaged seeds than the lightest third, weighing less than 393 g (Table 4). Heavy cones averaged 112.9 healthy kernels per cone, compared with 76.1 in light cones—a 48.4% higher count. But nut count per cone is different from cone-to-pine-nut yield (PY): the weight of dried healthy kernels as a percentage of fresh cone weight. This edible share was 4.05% in heavy cones and 3.62% in light cones. That is a difference of 0.43 percentage points, or 11.9% more edible kernel weight for the same fresh cone weight. These results describe the nut content of cones, not the total harvest per tree or plantation. Seeds in their shells made up 19.8% of fresh cone weight in heavy cones, compared with 18.7% in light cones. However, healthy kernels made up 20.9% versus 20.3% of seed-with-shell weight, and the statistical test did not establish a clear difference for this separate measure (Table 4). The edible share reflects both how much seed material a cone contains and how much healthy edible material those seeds hold. Cone weight may help assess cone quality, but the observed link does not prove that making cones heavier will raise the edible share. Added water through irrigation or plant nutrients through fertilization could encourage heavier cones. However, our study did not test these practices as controlled treatments. The findings support testing these approaches, rather than treating them as established ways to improve the edible share.

New or changed glossary terms

  • Pinus pinea → stone pine: The pine species studied here, whose seeds contain edible pine nuts.
  • plantation → area of planted trees: A place where trees have been planted, rather than a forest established entirely through natural growth.
  • seed; in-shell pine nut → seed in its shell: The whole seed, including its hard shell. A counted seed may contain a healthy kernel, a damaged kernel or no developed kernel.
  • pine nut; kernel → edible part inside the seed: The part eaten after the shell is removed. Only healthy kernels contributed to the study’s healthy-kernel count and edible yield.
  • cone-to-pine-nut yield (PY) → edible share: Healthy-kernel weight after drying, divided by fresh cone weight and multiplied by 100. It measures edible weight per cone weight, not the percentage of seeds that are healthy.
  • extreme tertiles → lightest and heaviest thirds: The two end groups formed when cone weights are divided into thirds. The study compared these groups rather than including the middle third.
  • mixed linear model → statistical comparison accounting for plantations and years: A method used here to compare heavy and light cones while allowing for variation linked to where and when cones were collected.
  • regression tree → analysis that repeatedly splits observations into groups: A method that chooses measurements and dividing values to separate groups with different average outcomes. Its branches describe patterns, not proof of cause and effect.
  • cone-to-seed yield (SY) → seed-with-shell share of cone weight: The weight of seeds including their shells, expressed as a percentage of fresh cone weight.
  • seed-to-pine-nut yield (SPY) → healthy-kernel share of seed-with-shell weight: The weight of healthy dried kernels, expressed as a percentage of the total weight of seeds including their shells.
  • percentage-point difference → difference between two percentages: Subtracting 3.62% from 4.05% gives a difference of 0.43 percentage points.
  • relative percentage increase → increase compared with the starting value: The difference divided by the comparison value, multiplied by 100. Here, 0.43 divided by 3.62 and multiplied by 100 is about 11.9%.
  • bioactive compounds → substances that affect processes in the body: Chemical substances that can influence biological activity. The term alone does not establish a particular health benefit.
  • inherited traits → features that can pass from parents to offspring: Characteristics linked to genetic information, which can be considered when selecting trees for crop development.
  • domestication → making a species better suited to farming: A process involving selection of useful inherited features, together with development of suitable growing practices.
  • irrigation → adding water: Supplying water to plants in addition to the water they receive naturally.
  • fertilization → adding plant nutrients: Supplying nutrients that plants need to grow, usually through fertilizer.
