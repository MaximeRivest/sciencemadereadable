People planted stone pine outside its natural range—the places where the species grows naturally—as far back as 2,800 years ago [5]. Opportunities to sell pine nuts have helped expand stone pine farming to countries outside that range, including Argentina [6], Australia [7], New Zealand [8] and Chile [9]. Major efforts are being made to increase pine nut production [10]. This matters because edible kernels make up only a very small part of a cone’s weight [11]. The amount of pine nuts harvested varies greatly from year to year [12]. It is linked to the number of cones and to measurements such as the sizes and weights of cones, seeds and kernels. These features help determine cone-to-pine-nut yield (PY), which we call the **edible share**. This expresses healthy kernel weight as a percentage of cone weight. In our study, kernels were weighed after drying, while cones were weighed fresh at harvest. Reports from Europe describe steep declines in two measures of yield in recent decades. Cone-to-seed yield is the weight of seeds, including their shells, as a percentage of cone weight. This fell from 17% to 5%, while the edible share fell from 4% to 2% or even less. Even healthy-looking cones could contain up to 50% empty seeds—seeds with no developed kernel inside [13]. Businesses buy cones by weight, not by their edible nut content [14]. Tracking the edible share has therefore become more important as empty and damaged seeds have become more common [15]. These seed problems have been linked to the insect *Leptoglossus occidentalis* [16, 17] and to drought [13, 17]. This insect was detected in Chile in 2017 [18, 19] and in Argentina in 2019 [20]. Earlier studies found links between cone weight and the number and weight of pine nuts [21, 22]. However, a study in Chile did not find clear statistical evidence of a link between cone weight and the edible share [23]. That result left the relationship uncertain, rather than proving there was no link. Larger cones have also been linked to better harvest quality [21]. Fertilization (adding plant nutrients) and irrigation (adding water) could encourage trees to produce heavier cones. These practices might also increase the edible share, but that possible benefit needs testing. We aimed to compare the number of damaged seeds and the edible share between heavy and light stone pine cones across a wide range of places and years in Chile. Our prediction was that heavy cones would have a larger edible share and a lower percentage of damaged seeds than light cones. Understanding the edible share is important for expanding stone pine farming in Chile. Comparing harvest measurements with values reported for stone pine in other parts of the world is also useful to the international pine nut industry and related organizations.

New or changed glossary terms

  • native distribution range → natural range: The places where a species occurs naturally, rather than being introduced by people.
  • inter-annual variability → changes from year to year: Differences between years; here, changes in the amount of pine nuts produced.
  • morphometric characters → size and weight measurements: Measured physical features of cones, seeds and kernels, such as length, width and weight.
  • empty seeds → seeds with no developed kernel inside: Seeds that contain no developed edible kernel, as distinct from seeds containing a damaged kernel.
  • Leptoglossus occidentalis → insect linked to seed damage: The insect species named in the cited research as a contributor to empty or damaged seeds.
  • non-significant correlation → no clear statistical evidence of a link: A statistical test did not establish a clear relationship between two measurements. This does not prove that no relationship exists.
  • spatial-temporal variability → differences among places and years: Variation associated with where and when cones were collected.
  • working hypothesis → prediction to test: The researchers’ expectation about how heavy and light cones would differ. A prediction is not itself a finding.
