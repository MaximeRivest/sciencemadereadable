Results Across plantations and years, cone weight was on average 470 g, with an average PY of 3.91%. No significant interaction between cone weight category and plantation (p>0.05) was found for any trait. Weight of light and heavy cones was below 393 g and above 503 g, respectively. Compared with the light cone category, heavy cones had a 40.0% higher number of seeds per cone (125 vs 89 seeds per cone, respectively), 26.3% higher seed weight, larger seeds (13.3% and 9.5% greater length and width, respectively) and 5.8% higher cone to seed yield. The heavy cone weight category also had a 48.4% higher number of pine nuts per cone (112.9 vs 76.1 pine nuts per cone, respectively), 22.2% higher pine nut weight, 13.0% longer pine nuts, 4.1% wider pine nuts, 11.9% higher PY (4.05 vs 3.62%) and a lower number of damaged seeds (9.0% vs 15.9% damaged seeds) (Table 4). Earlier conversation rewrite

### How heavy and light cones differed Across the plantations and years, cones weighed about 470 grams on average. Edible pine nuts made up an average of 3.91% of cone weight—about 3.91 grams of food for every 100 grams of cone. The light-cone group contained cones weighing less than 393 grams. The heavy-cone group contained cones weighing more than 503 grams. We did not find clear evidence that the differences between heavy and light cones changed from one plantation to another. This does not prove that the pattern was exactly the same everywhere, but our tests did not detect a clear difference between sites. ### Seeds inside their shells Heavy cones contained about 125 seeds each, compared with 89 in light cones—a difference of 40.0%. The seeds in heavy cones were also larger. On average, they were: - **26.3% heavier** - **13.3% longer** - **9.5% wider** Seeds, including their shells, also made up a larger share of the heavy cones’ weight. That share was 5.8% higher than in light cones. ### Edible pine nuts Heavy cones contained about 113 healthy, edible pine nuts each, compared with 76 in light cones. This was a difference of 48.4%. The edible nuts from heavy cones were, on average: - **22.2% heavier** - **13.0% longer** - **4.1% wider** Edible nuts made up **4.05% of the weight of heavy cones**, compared with **3.62% for light cones**. In other words, the same weight of heavy cones provided 11.9% more edible nuts by weight than light cones. Heavy cones also had a smaller share of empty or damaged seeds: **9.0%, compared with 15.9%** in light cones. Table 4 gives the full measurements and shows the uncertainty around the estimated averages. ### Which features were linked to more edible nuts? Our branching comparison, shown in Figure 1, first separated cones by the number of healthy pine nuts they contained. For cones with **78 or fewer nuts**, edible nuts made up an average of **2.60% of cone weight**. For cones with **79 or more**, the average was **4.04%**. The first group therefore provided about 35.6% less edible nut weight for the same weight of cones. Among cones with fewer nuts, there was another clear split: - Cones with **45 or fewer nuts** provided an average of **1.83%** of their weight as edible nuts. - Cones with **46 to 78 nuts** provided an average of **3.02%**. The first of these groups had a 39.4% lower share of edible nuts than the second. For cones containing **79 or more nuts**, the amount of edible nut inside the seed shells also mattered. Where edible nuts made up more than 21.0% of the seeds’ total weight, including shells, they accounted for an average of **4.54% of cone weight**. Where that share was 21.0% or less, the average was **3.64%**. Within the group above 21.0%, there was one more useful split. Where seeds with their shells made up more than 20.8% of cone weight, edible nuts accounted for **5.10% of cone weight**. Where seeds made up 20.8% or less, the figure was **4.35%**. These comparisons show which features went together with a greater share of edible nuts. They do not, on their own, show what would happen if a grower changed how the trees were managed. ### Differences between plantations Figure 2 shows how cone weight, individual pine nut weight, the number of healthy nuts, and the number of damaged seeds varied at each plantation between 2017 and 2020.
