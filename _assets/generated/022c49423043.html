Discussion Size is an important quality attribute in most fruit crops [30]. In stone pine, the relationship between cone quality and cone weight was analyzed; size and cone weight were found to be statistically correlated and cone weight was also correlated with seed and pine nut weight [23, 31]. Cone weight has shown to be affected by climatic conditions in the spring of the last year of cone maturation, especially by rainfall [32, 33], which partially explains the high inter-annual variability previously reported in the species’ native habitat [34]. In Chile [9], annual rainfall [35] and average temperature were also positively correlated with cone weight. There is a known dependence of PY on number of pine nuts inside cones [20]; the relationship of PY and cone weight is becoming a cause of concern due to the effect of pests and diseases on seeds. In this study, cone weight was on average 470 g, which is higher than in the species’ native range [34, 36–40]. In the 2010–2020 period in Chile, seed to pine nut yield was on average 20.3% across sites and years; these results are in line with values reported for the species’ native range [36, 41–43]. Similarly, average PY value (3.9%) is also within the range of historical values reported for Italy (3.6% [40, 44] and Spain (2.7–4.4% [45]). However, the average PY value is higher than values reported for Europe after the arrival of L. occidentalis, which caused a drastic decrease in Spain (1.1–2.1% [31, 45]) and Portugal (1.7% [46]; 3.0% [44]). In Chile, a trend towards PY decrease was also reported [23, 47]. Regarding cone weight, our results showed that the PY value of the heavy cone category was 11.9% higher than that of the light category across sites and years, in agreement with [36]. This result disagrees with findings that showed no effect of cone weight on PY in the species’ native habitat [32]. This PY increase in the heavy cone category is explained by the increase in the number of healthy pine nuts per cone (48.4%) and pine nut weight (22.2%), the most influential variables in determining PY [23]. The measured values of pine nuts per cone (cone filling) are higher than those reported for Turkey [48], Italy [49] and Portugal [22], leading to an increased production. The fact that PY is higher in heavier fruits has been reported for other crops [50–52], with composed fruits, formed from one flower and containing several seeds, as occurs in stone pine. In agreement with previous findings [53], the number of damaged seeds was significantly lower in the heavy cone than in the light cone weight category, which favors PY. The average proportion of damaged seeds (12.8%) is similar to that reported for Croatia [54] and lower than those reported for Tunisia (19.3% [55]), Spain (50% [56], 60% [57]) and Lebanon (60% [58]). However, the average number of damaged seeds was twice as high as the value reported for Chile in 2018 [59]. This difference could be due to damage by L. occidentalis and/or to the severe uninterrupted mega-drought that has affected the country since 2010, with rainfall deficits of up to 40% [60]. The distribution of PY-related variables for Quilvo, Cáhuil and Rosario–geographically close locations with a similar rainfall regime–is not enough to attribute the observed differences to the irrigation provided in Quilvo. Cone weight is highly variable within plantations; therefore, the differences in PY between the heaviest and the lightest cones are not trivial. However, our results indicate that cone weight may be used as an indicator of stone pine cone quality, in agreement with [36, 61]. In fact, our study showed that bigger cones contain a higher number of seeds (unshelled pine nuts), higher yield and bigger pine nuts (shelled white pine nuts, the edible component), as previously reported [36]. This longitudinal multi-environment study showed the dependence of PY on cone weight; hence, management practices, such as fertilization and irrigation, could be used to boost production of heavy cones. In fact, previous studies reported that fertilization increased PY [61] and cone weight [62, 63]. The benefits of fertilization with both micronutrients [64, 65] and macronutrients [21, 38] have been studied in adult stone pine trees. On the other hand, irrigation has also been found to improve cone weight in stone pine [61, 66, 67]. Therefore, further studies targeting these and other management practices to increase cone weight should explore tools to boost PY, with the consequent economic benefits.

## Discussion ### Comparisons with earlier studies Size is an important part of quality in many fruit crops [30]. Earlier studies of stone pine found that heavier cones tended to be larger and to contain heavier seeds and kernels [23, 31]. Other research linked cone weight to spring weather in the final year of cone development, especially rainfall [32, 33]. These weather differences may help explain why cone weight varies greatly from year to year in the species’ natural range [34]. Research in Chile also linked greater annual rainfall and higher average temperatures with heavier cones [9]. Understanding how cone weight relates to edible share has become more important because pests and diseases can damage seeds. The number of healthy kernels matters: it is one part of the edible-share calculation. Across our sites and sampled years in 2010–2020, cones averaged about 474 g. This was higher than weights reported for stone pine’s natural range [34, 36–40]. Healthy dried kernels made up an average of 20.3% of the total weight of seeds in their shells. This seed-based share was similar to values reported from the natural range [36, 41–43]. Our average **edible share** was 3.9% of fresh cone weight. This was close to a historical value of 3.6% for Italy [40, 44] and within the historical range of 2.7–4.4% for Spain [45]. It was higher, however, than European values reported after the arrival of the insect *L. occidentalis*: 1.1–2.1% in Spain [31, 45], and 1.7% [46] and 3.0% [44] in Portugal. European reports have linked large declines in edible share to this insect. Earlier studies have also reported a downward trend in edible share in Chile [23, 47]. These international comparisons use values from separate studies. They are not experiments in which growing conditions were held the same across countries, so they do not establish why the values differ. ### What cone weight may tell us Across plantations and years, the heaviest third of cones had an edible share of 4.05%, compared with 3.62% in the lightest third. The difference was 0.43 percentage points, or an 11.9% relative increase over the light-cone value. This agrees with earlier research [36], but differs from a report from the natural range that found no clear link between cone weight and edible share [32]. As noted in the Introduction, earlier Chilean work also left this link uncertain [23]. The larger edible share in heavy cones went along with 48.4% more healthy kernels per cone and 22.2% greater weight per kernel. These two measurements are key parts of the edible-share calculation and were identified as important in earlier research [23]. Heavy cones also contained more seeds in their shells and had larger kernels (Table 4), matching earlier findings [36]. Our measured number of healthy kernels per cone—also called **cone filling**—was higher than values reported for Turkey [48], Italy [49] and Portugal [22]. More kernels in each cone does not, by itself, establish a larger total harvest per tree or plantation. Similar links between heavier fruits or other seed-holding parts and higher yields have been reported for other crops [50–52]. Cone weights vary greatly within plantations, so the heavy–light difference in edible share could matter in practice. Our findings suggest that cone weight may help indicate cone quality, as earlier work also suggested [36, 61]. ### Seed damage and possible explanations The statistical comparison supported a lower percentage of empty or damaged seeds in heavy cones: 9.0%, compared with 15.9% in light cones (Table 4). This agrees with earlier research [53]. The lower damage percentage occurred alongside the heavy cones’ larger edible share. Across sites and years, the average reported percentage of empty or damaged seeds was 12.8%. This was similar to the percentage reported for Croatia [54] and lower than values reported for Tunisia (19.3% [55]), Spain (50% [56] and 60% [57]) and Lebanon (60% [58]). However, our average damaged-seed count was reported as twice the value in a Chilean study published in 2018 [59]. This difference could reflect damage by *L. occidentalis*, the severe, long-lasting drought that began in central Chile in 2010, or both. During this **mega-drought**, rainfall was reported to be as much as 40% below usual levels [60]. Our study did not establish whether either factor explains the difference in seed damage. ### What to test next The site comparisons limit what we can conclude about irrigation. Quilvo, Cáhuil and Rosario are geographically close and have similar rainfall patterns. Quilvo received added water through irrigation. However, the differences in cone, seed and kernel measurements among these sites (Figure 2) are not enough to show that irrigation caused those differences. Fertilization and irrigation remain possible ways to encourage heavier cones. Earlier studies reported a larger edible share with fertilization [61], as well as greater cone weight [62, 63]. Research on mature stone pine trees has examined adding nutrients needed in small amounts, called **micronutrients** [64, 65], and nutrients needed in larger amounts, called **macronutrients** [21, 38]. Earlier work also reported heavier stone pine cones with irrigation [61, 67]. These are findings from previous studies, not treatment effects established by our measurements. Our observations across several places and years show a link between cone weight and edible share. They do not prove that making cones heavier will increase that share, and we did not run a controlled test of irrigation or fertilization. Further studies should test these and other growing practices to find out whether they increase cone weight and edible share, and whether any gains bring financial benefits for growers.

New or changed glossary terms

  • cone filling → number of healthy kernels in a cone: Here, this refers to healthy-kernel count per cone. It is different from the edible share, which compares kernel weight with fresh cone weight.
  • mega-drought → severe drought lasting many years: A prolonged period with much less rainfall than usual. The cited research describes a drought in central Chile that began in 2010.
  • micronutrients → plant nutrients needed in small amounts: Nutrients that plants require in relatively small quantities. The name refers to the amount needed, not to whether the nutrients are important.
  • macronutrients → plant nutrients needed in larger amounts: Nutrients that plants require in relatively large quantities compared with micronutrients. Both groups are needed for plant growth.
