What is inside

A heavier cone can hold more nuts. But does it give you more food for the same weight of cones? Researchers in Chile investigated.

First, what are we weighing?

The whole cone, the seed with its shell, and the edible nut are three different things. Only the last one is the food we are counting.

06 /

A finding is not yet a farming instruction.

What the study found

Heavier cones tended to provide more edible nuts for the same weight of cones.

They also contained more healthy nuts, and a smaller share of their seeds were empty or damaged.

What still needs testing

Would extra watering or nutrients reliably improve the harvest?

This study did not directly test that cause-and-effect question. Growers would need evidence that a change works—and that the extra food is worth the cost.

Accuracy notes and design choices
  • “Edible share” compares dried edible nuts with freshly weighed whole cones. It is a food-output measure, not a picture of the cone’s fresh contents.
  • Table 4 supplies the averages and standard errors for the two weight groups. We preserve those uncertainties rather than drawing invented distributions.
  • The text beside the original branching chart appears to swap 2.60% and 4.04%. This companion follows Figure 1 itself: 78 or fewer nuts → 2.60%; 79 or more → 4.04%.
  • Half-number cutoffs in the original figure become whole-number groups here: ≤78.5 means 78 or fewer nuts; ≤45.5 means 45 or fewer. Other cutoffs remain exactly as reported.
  • The original Figure 2 shows distributions. We have not redrawn those box plots from averages: their underlying values are not available in the materials used here.
  • Bars share a zero baseline; circles use explicit counts and symbols. Meaning does not depend on colour, and the diagrams do not suggest that watering caused the observed differences.
